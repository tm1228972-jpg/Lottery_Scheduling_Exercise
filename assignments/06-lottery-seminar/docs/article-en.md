## Abstract

Lottery Scheduling is a randomized proportional-share allocation mechanism. Each runnable process owns a positive number of tickets; a uniformly drawn ticket selects the next process. If process `i` owns `tᵢ` of a total `T`, its probability of winning one independent draw is `tᵢ/T` [1, Sec. 2.2]. With a fixed ready set, the number of wins over repeated draws has a binomial distribution. The mechanism therefore targets an expected share, not an exact finite-trace ratio or a job deadline. This article explains the algorithm, its variance and first-win waiting model, ticket-management abstractions, and its difference from EDF and RM. It concludes with a 50,000-draw result generated by the repository implementation, including its seed and limitations.

**Keywords:** Lottery Scheduling, proportional share, EDF, Rate Monotonic, ticket, CPU scheduling.

## 1. Problem and motivation

A scheduler chooses among runnable processes. A proportional-share policy aims to distribute a resource according to long-term weights or claims; a real-time policy aims to complete jobs by deadlines. Lottery Scheduling addresses the former objective. A sample average close to a ticket ratio does not prove that a particular job will meet its deadline.

This difference matters in practice. A batch process, interactive request, or group of clients may be assigned relative shares. A control task, in contrast, may require a worst-case completion guarantee. Choosing the lottery by ticket weight does not encode a deadline ordering or a processor-demand test. The service contract must be stated before a scheduler is selected.

## 2. Algorithm and one-draw probability

Process `i` receives a positive integer `tᵢ` tickets; `T=Σtᵢ` is the total active pool. A draw selects a uniform integer in `[1,T]`, and cumulative ticket intervals identify its owner.

```text
LOTTERY(ready):
    require ready is non-empty
    T ← Σ process.tickets
    k ← uniform_integer(1, T)
    cumulative ← 0
    for process in ready:
        cumulative ← cumulative + process.tickets
        if k ≤ cumulative:
            dispatch process
            return process
```

For a fixed ready set and independent draws, process `i` wins one draw with probability `pᵢ=tᵢ/T`. The `tᵢ` tickets owned by `i` are `tᵢ` equally likely winning outcomes out of `T`; the cumulative intervals partition all `T` outcomes exactly once. With weights `(1,2,4)`, the total is 7 and the intervals are `[1,1]`, `[2,3]`, and `[4,7]`.

## 3. Expected share, variance, and waiting time

Across `n` independent draws with a fixed ready set and fixed ticket pool, the count `Xᵢ` of wins by process `i` is binomial:

`E[Xᵢ]=npᵢ`, `Var(Xᵢ)=npᵢ(1-pᵢ)`.

The expected share is the ticket ratio, but finite samples vary around it. Waldspurger and Weihl derive the binomial win-count model and note that the discrepancy between observed and expected proportions decreases as the number of allocations increases [1, Sec. 2.2]. This does not imply that every finite prefix matches the expected share.

For fixed `p>0`, the first-win opportunity is geometrically distributed. The probability of no win in `k` draws is `(1-p)^k`; the expected number of draws up to and including the first win is `1/p` [1, Sec. 2.2]. For every finite `k`, the no-win probability remains positive. Its limit is zero as `k→∞`, so eventual selection has probability one in the ideal infinite independent sequence, but there is no deterministic finite waiting bound. The original paper's “eventually” statement should not be read as a bounded-wait or hard-deadline guarantee.

If the ready set changes, ticket ownership changes, or the draws are dependent, the fixed-`p` binomial and geometric calculations no longer describe the trace without additional analysis.

## 4. Ticket-management abstractions

The Lottery Scheduling literature describes ticket transfer, ticket inflation, currencies, and compensation tickets [1, Sec. 3]. Transfer temporarily lends tickets across a dependency, such as a client waiting for an RPC server. Inflation changes a resource claim and therefore needs an authorization rule; unconstrained inflation would allow a participant to alter its relative share. Currency represents claims within a group or hierarchy and maps the group's share to a common allocation. Compensation tickets adjust effective weight when a client releases an unused part of its quantum.

These abstractions are part of the published design, not the repository's implementation. The code here models only fixed ticket counts in one fixed ready set; it does not implement transfer, inflation, currency, compensation, blocking, or dynamic arrival/departure.

## 5. Comparison with EDF and RM

| Policy | Selection key | Objective | Main result or limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lottery | Random ticket from ready set | Expected proportional share | Weight changes probability; no deadline or finite-wait bound |
| EDF | Earliest absolute deadline | Deadline satisfaction | Under ideal independent preemptive uniprocessor implicit-deadline assumptions, `U≤1` characterizes feasibility [2] |
| RM | Shortest period, fixed priority | Periodic fixed-priority scheduling | `n(2^(1/n)-1)` is a sufficient, not necessary, utilization bound [2] |

The classical EDF/RM results require assumptions such as independent tasks, preemption, one processor, implicit deadlines, and zero overhead [2]. Blocking, jitter, constrained deadlines, multiprocessors, non-preemption, and context-switch costs require additional analysis. Lottery, EDF, and RM should not be compared only by “fairness” or “speed”; first state the objective, workload, and system model.

## 7. Benefits, limitations, and responsible use

Ticket weights provide a simple relative-share control, and the abstraction can be studied for resources beyond CPUs [1]. In contrast, each draw is randomized, short-run allocation varies, and outcomes depend on the ticket-allocation policy. The current code does not implement hard real-time deadlines, bounded waiting, process blocking/wakeup, multicore operation, context-switch cost, or dynamic ready sets.

A positive ticket gives a positive chance on a draw; under the ideal fixed-`p` infinite model the process eventually wins with probability one, but a finite wait can be arbitrarily long. Do not claim that Lottery “eliminates starvation” without specifying which probabilistic liveness property is intended. Likewise, an EDF/RM task-set theorem is only as strong as its assumptions. Fairness, deadline satisfaction, response time, and throughput should be reported as separate measures.
